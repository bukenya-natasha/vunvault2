Module one

What is cybersecurity?

Press play to hear this lesson, then read and take the check. You need 2 of 3 to unlock the next module.

Cybersecurity is the practice of protecting computers, networks, applications and data from digital attacks, unauthorised access, damage or theft. It covers the password on your phone, hospital records, and the rails that move money.

Think of home security — locks, lights, a safe — applied to the digital world. A burglar must be at your door. A cybercriminal can attack from anywhere, unseen.

Why Africa is on the front line

Mobile money, digital banking and cloud tools brought millions into the formal economy. The same platforms are targets.

$4B+Lost to cybercrime in Africa yearly
32M+Active mobile-money users in Kenya
70%Of African SMEs have no security plan

These numbers are meant to motivate, not frighten. People who know the basics are far less likely to become victims.

What it actually protects

Your money — wallets, banks, investments. Your identity — national ID, passport, biometrics. Your business — customers, secrets, operations. Your reputation — trust that took years to build.

Takeaway. Cybersecurity is personal safety, business continuity and national security. If you use a phone, you are a target and a defender.

Module two

The cyber threat landscape

Phishing is the most common attack: someone pretends to be a bank, a company or a friend and tricks you into handing over a PIN or password.

Real example, Kenya. “Congratulations! Your M-Pesa won KSh 50,000. Click and enter your PIN before midnight.” Safaricom never asks for your PIN this way.

Phishing

Fake messages that steal credentials or money.

Malware

Software that spies, steals or damages a device.

Ransomware

Encrypts files and demands payment.

Social engineering

Psychology, not code, to make you act.

SIM swapping

Moving your number onto an attacker’s SIM.

Man-in-the-middle

Quietly reading traffic between two parties.

When ransomware hit a hospital

In 2020 Life Healthcare in South Africa had records and admissions encrypted. Care went back to paper. Recovery took weeks. Healthcare, finance and government here are now regular targets.

Social engineering uses urgency, authority, fear and fake favours. Spot the trigger and you break the attack.

Module three

The CIA triad

Confidentiality

Only authorised people can see the data — your balance is not public.

Integrity

The number has not been silently changed in transit.

Availability

Systems work when needed — hospital records at 3 a.m.

Send mobile money and all three apply: only you and the recipient should see it; the amount must not change; the service must be up when you need to pay school fees.

Ask of every control: does it protect confidentiality, integrity and availability? A “no” is a gap.

Module four

Passwords and authentication

Stolen credentials start a huge share of breaches. Of 19 billion leaked passwords studied in 2024–25, only 6% were unique. Reuse means one leak opens every other account.

password123 is weak. Nairobi2026! is medium. A long random string from a manager is strong.

Use a password manager — Bitwarden, Google Password Manager or Apple Keychain. Then add MFA. Microsoft’s research: MFA blocks over 99% of identity attacks even when the password is already stolen.

  1. Enable MFA on email first — it resets everything else.
  2. Then banking and mobile money.
  3. Prefer an authenticator app over SMS (SIM-swap can steal SMS codes).
  4. Print backup codes and store them offline.

Module five

Safe browsing and online habits

https:// and a padlock mean the connection is encrypted — not that the site is honest. Phishing sites use HTTPS too. Read the full domain: safaricom.co.ke is not safar1com.co.ke.

Red flags: urgency, PIN requests, misspelled domains, surprise WhatsApp links, “download this to verify.”

Do not open banking or M-Pesa on café or airport Wi-Fi without a VPN. Proton VPN is a reputable free start. Install apps only from official stores — never a “bonus M-Pesa app” from chat.

Module six

Device and mobile security

Your phone is wallet, ID and bank. Install updates — they patch holes attackers already use. Audit app permissions. Lock every device; encryption usually follows the lock screen.

If the phone is stolen

  1. Change email and mobile-money passwords from another device first.
  2. Call the network and lock the SIM so it cannot be swapped.
  3. Use Find My Device to locate, lock or erase.
  4. Get a police abstract for insurance and dispute fraud.

Module seven

Data protection and privacy

Name, ID, date of birth, phone and habits can be stitched into a fraud profile. Brokers buy this data. Criminals steal it.

In Kenya the Data Protection Act 2019 requires consent, a stated purpose, security, and deletion when you no longer need the data. Fines can reach KSh 5 million; officers can face prison.

3-2-1 backups: three copies, two kinds of storage, one copy off-site or in the cloud. That is how you recover from ransomware without paying.

Tighten social privacy. Work, home area and routines help attackers write convincing lures.

Module eight

When things go wrong

Warning signs: logins from unknown places, messages you did not send, a phone that suddenly has no service (possible SIM swap), money you did not send, apps you did not install.

The first hour

  1. Do not panic. Work the list.
  2. Disconnect the device (Wi-Fi and mobile data off).
  3. Change passwords from a clean device — email, then mobile money, then banking.
  4. Call the bank or M-Pesa immediately. Fast reports sometimes reverse a transfer.
  5. Screenshot everything.
  6. Report: Kenya DCI Cybercrime Unit · Nigeria EFCC · South Africa SAPS 10111 · Interpol.
