Kenya's State House Website Was Hacked and Held for Ransom, Again

28 July 2026 VUNVAULT Team 5 min read

In the early hours of Saturday, 18 July 2026, the official website of Kenya's presidency, president.go.ke, stopped showing its usual mix of speeches, press releases and policy updates. In their place sat a message aimed directly at President William Ruto, a Bitcoin wallet address, and a deadline. Whoever was behind it wanted five bitcoins, worth roughly KSh41.3 million at the time, paid by six that evening, or they said they'd release information about the president.

Journalists at Kenyans.co.ke were the first to spot the defacement and report it. Within hours, State House confirmed the breach, saying its ICT team was working with the National Kenya Computer Incident Response Team Coordination Centre to restrict access to the site while they figured out what had actually happened. ICT Cabinet Secretary William Kabogo posted on social media that the agencies responsible for protecting government websites were on top of the situation, though officials stopped short of saying whether the attackers had reached anything beyond the homepage.

Not the first time

This wasn't president.go.ke's first brush with attackers. In November 2025, a coordinated attack briefly knocked several government websites offline at once, including the presidency portal alongside ministries covering health, education, labour, environment, tourism, interior and ICT. That earlier incident was also resolved through emergency response procedures, and the government said at the time that no sensitive data had been accessed. This made July's breach the second to hit the presidential site in under a year.

By Wednesday, 22 July, president.go.ke was back to normal, with speeches, statements and policy pages all reachable again. Officials have since said they see no evidence of a data breach, though they've stayed quiet on whether the ransom itself was ever paid or seriously considered.

What this keeps proving

Whether or not the attackers actually had anything to leak, incidents like this rarely need to succeed technically to do real damage. A defaced homepage is public within minutes. Screenshots spread faster than any official statement can catch up to. The story quickly becomes less about what data actually moved and more about whether an institution can keep its own front door locked.

That's true whether the target is a presidency or a small business site. The mechanics tend to be the same in both cases: an exposed login, an outdated plugin, weak session handling, or a content management system nobody has patched in a while.

Why it matters beyond government

Kenya has invested heavily in digitizing government services, and for citizens that's genuinely a good thing. But every service that moves online is one more surface an attacker can try. Whether you're running a government portal or a small ecommerce site, the basics are still what stop most of these attacks: patched software, monitored logins, a real incident response plan you've thought through before you need it, not after.

If your business has a website with an admin login, a content management system, or a customer portal, it's worth asking the same question State House had to answer twice this year: if someone got in tonight, would you even know until they told you?
