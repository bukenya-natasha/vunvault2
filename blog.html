Canadian Man Pleads Guilty in the Snowflake Extortion Scheme That Hit 165+ Companies

Updated Aug 2026 Reading Time 6 Minutes Author VUNVAULT Security Team
The Snowflake extortion campaign breached more than 165 organizations and exposed data tied to over 100 million people.

A 26-year-old Canadian man once described as one of the most consequential cybercrime threat actors of 2024 has pleaded guilty to a sprawling hacking and extortion conspiracy that targeted more than 165 organizations using the cloud platform Snowflake.

Connor Riley Moucka, of Kitchener, Ontario, entered the plea in Seattle federal court on Wednesday, August 5, 2026, admitting to computer fraud, wire fraud, aggravated identity theft, and a related conspiracy. He also admitted to stealing the call and text history records of more than 100 million AT&T customers.

One of the Most Consequential Hackers of 2024

Moucka, known online under the aliases "Waifu" and "Judische", was described by Google's Mandiant as one of the most consequential threat actors of 2024. Investigators track the campaign under the moniker UNC5537. Between February and October 2024, Moucka and his co-conspirators used stolen login credentials to compromise cloud-hosted data belonging to at least 165 customers of a U.S.-based software-as-a-service provider.

Publicly identified victims included AT&T, Ticketmaster, Santander Bank, Advance Auto Parts, Neiman Marcus, LendingTree's QuoteWizard, Ticketek, Pure Storage, the Los Angeles Unified School District, and Bausch Health.

"Connor Moucka hacked over 150 companies and organizations, obtained extremely sensitive information, and extorted the victims for millions of dollars." — Assistant Attorney General A. Tysen Duva, DOJ Criminal Division

Billions of Records, Terabytes of Data

The conspirators stole billions of sensitive customer records and downloaded terabytes of information, including:

  • Non-content call and text history records (about 50 billion records from AT&T alone)
  • Banking and other financial information
  • Payroll records
  • Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) registration numbers
  • Driver's license numbers, passport numbers, and Social Security numbers

According to prosecutors, the scheme generated more than $2.5 million in ransom payments, with Moucka personally obtaining at least $495,000 from ransoms and data sales on cybercrime forums including BreachForums, Exploit.in, and XSS.is, as well as via Telegram.

'Calculated and Predatory' Re-Extortion

Perhaps the most disturbing element of the case is that Moucka and his accomplices returned to squeeze at least one victim a second time. After a victim paid a ransom in May 2024, the conspirators returned months later demanding more money, threatening to publish or sell the stolen customer data.

"Moucka used the stolen data of a government officer and members of a then-former government officer's immediate family in this re-extortion attempt," the Justice Department said. Moucka also threatened and harassed government officials and security researchers who were helping track him down.

FBI Special Agent in Charge W. Mike Herrington called Moucka's tactics "calculated and predatory," noting the real harm done to both targeted companies and "the millions of everyday people who are their customers."

Stolen data was advertised for sale on BreachForums, Exploit.in and XSS.is, and via Telegram.

Not Sophisticated — Just Negligent Credentials

Perhaps the most important lesson from the Snowflake campaign is that it did not rely on novel or sophisticated hacking techniques. Mandiant found that every incident it investigated traced back to customer credentials stolen by infostealer malware. Some had been harvested as far back as November 2020 and were still valid years later.

The campaign, Mandiant wrote, "is not the result of any particularly novel or sophisticated tool, technique, or procedure." It succeeded because of the size of the infostealer market and credentials left unrotated for as long as four years. Crucially, many Snowflake customer accounts did not enforce multi-factor authentication (MFA).

Snowflake responded by increasing password-complexity requirements and enforcing MFA. At least 79.7% of the accounts the group used had prior credential exposure, and compromised instances had no network allow lists.

The Co-Conspirators

Moucka was not alone. One admitted co-conspirator is Cameron "Kiberphant0m" Wagenius, a U.S. Army soldier who pleaded guilty in July 2025 to extorting AT&T and Verizon for customer account data. He is set to be sentenced on September 3, 2026.

The third alleged co-conspirator is John Erin Binns, 26, an elusive American indicted for his admitted role in a 2021 T-Mobile breach that exposed the personal information of at least 76 million customers. Binns has reportedly resurfaced online and obtained Turkish citizenship, under which he may not be extraditable.

Arrest, Extradition and Sentencing

Canadian law enforcement, acting on a U.S. arrest warrant, arrested Moucka in Ontario in November 2024 — just months after the breaches began. He agreed to be extradited to the United States, and appeared in Seattle federal court in July 2025.

Moucka pleaded guilty to four counts and is scheduled to be sentenced on October 27, 2026. He faces a mandatory minimum of two years on the aggravated identity-theft count and a maximum of 30 years in prison on the remaining counts. He has agreed to forfeit the money he made, and could be ordered to pay restitution for victim incident-response and ransom costs that totaled at least $9.5 million.

What this means for you: The Snowflake case is a stark reminder that many of the largest data breaches begin not with exotic exploits, but with stolen or reused credentials. Enable multi-factor authentication on every account that supports it, use a password manager to avoid reusing passwords, rotate credentials regularly, and restrict access with network allow lists. Africa's Cyber Awakening: How the Continent Is Fighting Back

Updated Jul 2026 Reading Time 8 Minutes Author VUNVAULT Security Team

Mobile money, deepfake voice notes, a skills gap the size of the Sahara, and a new generation of hackers who'd rather defend than destroy. This is the true tale of cybersecurity in Africa today and what it implies for you.

A quick gut check: would you recognise a phoney WhatsApp voicemail from your "bank manager" requesting you to confirm a transfer if it came from someone in Nairobi, Lagos, Accra, or Johannesburg? You might not today. To put it succinctly, cybersecurity in Africa has evolved from a back office IT issue to one of the continent's most crucial discussions.

We're going to take you on a tour of what's actually occurring in African cybersecurity right now: the positive, the frightening, and the unexpectedly optimistic. Here is a breakdown of our research:

Africa's cities are going digital fast, and the infrastructure is racing to keep up.

The Digital Boom in Africa has become a Playground for Hackers

The fact that no one tells you is that Africa's cybersecurity issue is a result of the continent's rapid success in another area: digitalisation. Fintech apps, mobile money wallets, telemedicine platforms, smart farming sensors, and government digital services are just a few examples of how the continent has surpassed desktop computers to become a mobile first, cloud first world.

However, each new digital ID card, sensor, and app is also a new door. Additionally, many of those doors are being installed more quickly than they are being locked. Analysts monitoring the region observe that the quick uptake of connected devices in Africa, ranging from health wearables to smart agriculture sensors, has created enormous attack surfaces that are frequently left unprotected, with many devices still coming with out of date software and weak default passwords.

Africa's cybersecurity personnel shortage is a structural issue that few foreign observers address beneath the surface of the continent's digital transformation.

  • Africa has the lowest per capita concentration of cybersecurity professionals worldwide, according to the International Telecommunication Union (ITU).
  • Over 100,000 qualified security specialists are needed throughout the continent, according to the African Union's cybersecurity expert committee. This is a failure of training pipelines, certification availability, and investment in cyber education rather than a lack of talent.

The result is striking. Within eighteen months, a fintech business in Lagos can introduce a mobile banking platform that serves two million users. However, it might only have one security engineer or none at all. Without a specialised incident response team, a government ministry in East Africa might digitise its whole land registry and implement a citizen facing portal. Although the doors are being constructed, there is a severe shortage of locksmiths. And there has been an explosion of Internet of Things (IoT) devices across Africa which represents one of the most significant and least discussed cybersecurity challenges on the continent.

Agriculture technology companies are deploying thousands of soil sensors, weather stations, and drone linked monitoring systems across vast rural areas. Healthcare providers are distributing wearable monitors to patients with chronic conditions. Logistics firms are fitting truck fleets with GPS trackers and fuel sensors. Smart meters are proliferating in countries modernizing their electricity grids.

Every one of these gadgets could be a point of entry. IoT related attacks on the region increased significantly between 2022 and 2024, according to research from cybersecurity company Kaspersky, which has a sizable research presence in Africa. The attack vectors were eerily familiar: unencrypted communication routes sending private information over public networks, firmware that was never upgraded, and default credentials that were never altered. The lifespan of these devices exacerbates the issue. Without any physical upkeep, a soil sensor placed on a farm in rural Rwanda might last for five to seven years. However, without over the air update mechanisms, a feature that is frequently removed to cut costs, that sensor is running the same firmware on its last day that it ran on its first, regardless of what vulnerabilities have been discovered in the intervening years.

The Mobile Money Attack Surface

Perhaps the most revolutionary digital breakthrough in Africa is mobile money. Hundreds of millions of people who never had bank accounts now have access to financial services because to platforms like MPesa in Kenya, MTN Mobile Money throughout West Africa, and Airtel Money in several countries. Over 70% of mobile money transactions worldwide occur in sub Saharan Africa alone, according to the GSMA's State of the Industry Report on Mobile Money.

However, the end user's handset is frequently the weakest link in the mobile money security architecture. SIM swap fraud has grown to be a multimillion dollar criminal enterprise throughout the continent. In this type of fraud, attackers socially engineer or bribe mobile network staff to move a victim's phone number to a SIM card under their control. The banking authority in South Africa has issued numerous warnings about SIM swap fraud losses. The Central Bank of Nigeria has issued many circulars asking financial institutions to abandon SMS based two factor authentication. This request recognises the extent to which insecure authentication has been ingrained in the financial infrastructure of the region.

In addition to SIM swap assaults, low cost Android devices are a major issue in African countries. These devices, which are frequently under $50, come with antiquated operating systems that are seldom updated for security. In the hands of users who depend on them for government interactions, banking, and health services, they become irreversible, unpatched vulnerabilities. Mobile apps from African countries showed disproportionately high rates of API key exposure and insufficient certificate pinning, which exposed financial transactions to eavesdropping and manipulation, according to a 2023 study by the research firm Approov.

Mobile money kiosks like these process a huge share of everyday transactions across the continent, and each one is a potential attack surface.

The Talent Drain and Its Consequences

The talent drain is another aspect of African cybersecurity that is rarely covered in Western media. Advanced certifications such as CISSP, OSCP, and SANS GIAC make African cybersecurity specialists instantly valuable to organisations in North America, Europe, and the Gulf States. Recruitment from foreign governments and businesses is active and well funded. As a result, the very specialists who should be strengthening Africa's defence are constantly leaving the continent.

Several African nations are taking action after realising this. Rwanda has created the Rwanda Information Society Authority to oversee national cyber defence and has made investments in cybersecurity education through collaborations with foreign universities. Kenya authorised cybersecurity training for government IT workers and established the National Computer and Cybercrimes Coordination Committee. Mauritius has established itself as a possible center for cybersecurity services. Despite their sincerity, these initiatives are being outpaced by the rapidity of digital adoption and the increasing complexity of threat actors.

Ransomware and the Public Sector Vulnerability

Ransomware operators are finding African public sector entities to be more appealing targets. Attacks that take advantage of the same basic flaws (underfunded IT departments, a lack of offline backups, unpatched systems, and a lack of incident response plans) have affected hospitals, municipal governments, and educational institutions around the continent. The public sector in South Africa has been particularly badly affected, with numerous high profile ransomware instances impacting state owned businesses, municipal services, and medical facilities. Millions of medical tests were not processed in 2023 due to a ransomware assault on the South African National Health Laboratory Service. Similar instances have been reported in Ghana, Kenya, and Nigeria.

The vulnerability of the systems being targeted is what makes these attacks particularly destructive in the African environment. When a hospital in a wealthy nation is attacked by ransomware, it can frequently use cash reserves to pay for repair, move patients to other institutions, or access redundant systems. The repercussions of a regional hospital in a developing nation losing access to its digital records can be fatal. There aren't any nearby facilities with extra space. No redundant systems exist. There isn't a financial safety net.

The Regulatory Response: Progress and Gaps

Africa's regulatory environment is changing quickly yet unevenly. The Malabo Convention, also known as the Convention on Cyber Security and Personal Data Protection, was adopted by the African Union in 2014, but it took over ten years for enough member states to ratify it before it became operative. Meanwhile, each nation has advanced at its own speed.

One of the most extensive data privacy laws on the continent is South Africa's privacy of Personal Information Act (POPIA), which went into full force in 2021. The Office of the Data Protection Commissioner was created by Kenya's 2019 Data Protection Act, which also introduced severe penalties for non compliance. Nigeria's Data Protection Act, signed into law in 2023, created the Nigeria Data Protection Commission and established a legal framework that aligns broadly with international standards.

However, the effectiveness of legislation depends on its enforcement, and most of the continent still lacks adequate enforcement. Many nations have enacted legislation pertaining to cybersecurity and data protection, but they lack the forensic tools, investigative resources, and judicial knowledge necessary to implement them. In some regions of Africa, a data breach that would result in regulatory scrutiny and possible fines in Europe can go completely unexamined, not because there is no law, but rather because the enforcement mechanism has not yet been established.

What the World Misses

When African cybersecurity is discussed at all in international forums, the prevailing narrative portrays the continent as either a source of cybercrime, especially the "Yahoo Boys" phenomenon in West Africa, or as a victim, a passive target of cybercriminals from more developed nations. Both frameworks lack completeness and are reductive.

In reality, the situation is more complicated and urgent. Africa is developing a digital economy in real time under circumstances that no other region has encountered: a large, youthful, and quickly connected population; a leapfrog trajectory that ignores legacy infrastructure; a cybersecurity expert shortage that cannot be swiftly resolved; and a threat landscape that includes both local criminal actors and some of the world's most advanced state sponsored organisations.

Making this right is important to the global cybersecurity community. Africa is not a remote island in the digital world. Swift and Visa are also connected to Kinshasa's mobile money services. Cloud services hosted in Virginia and Amsterdam are integrated with the fintech apps developed in Lagos. A flaw in the API of an African startup is a flaw in the international financial system. Targets in Singapore or London could be attacked by an IoT botnet that is recruited from unpatched smart meters in Johannesburg.

The Rise of the "Fake Voice Note": Deepfakes Go Mainstream

Remember when scam calls were easy to spot because of the weird robotic accent? Those days are over. Now, scammers can clone a real person's voice from a few seconds of audio, a voicemail greeting, a podcast clip, even a wedding speech, and use it to approve fake payments or trick a relative into "helping" in an emergency.

This is now considered one of the fastest growing threats in Africa's mobile first economy, with fraud losses tied to SIM swap and impersonation scams becoming a serious and worsening problem for South African banks in particular. Expect this trend to spread across borders, especially anywhere mobile banking is part of daily life, which, in Africa, is basically everywhere.

"If it sounds urgent, sounds like someone you trust, and asks you to move money fast, stop. Call them back on a number you already have saved."

It's Not Just Malware Anymore: Meet "Permission Drift"

Old school hacking movies love a hoodie typing furiously to "break through the firewall." The real threat today is way less dramatic and way more common: someone in the cloud team leaves an API open, a contractor keeps access to a system after their contract ends, or a setting gets flipped and nobody notices for months.

Security researchers now estimate that the majority of cloud security incidents across African organizations stem from these kinds of misconfigurations and unmonitored access points, not sophisticated malware. It's less Hollywood, more "left the back door unlocked."

What this means for your business

  • Review who has access to what: quarterly, not "whenever we remember"
  • Turn off accounts the moment someone leaves the team
  • Get a professional scan instead of guessing (this is, quite literally, what VunVault's Scanner service is for)

Ransomware Grows Up: From "Pay or Lose Your Files" to "Pay or We Corrupt Everything"

Ransomware used to be simple: hackers lock your files, you pay to get them back. The newer, meaner version doesn't just lock data, it quietly corrupts it. Imagine a power utility's grid data being subtly altered instead of stolen. The lights don't necessarily go out immediately, but the wrong decisions start getting made on bad information.

As industrial and infrastructure systems across Africa digitize at a fast pace, this shift from "steal and lock" to "corrupt and pressure" is becoming one of the more unsettling trends security teams are watching closely.

The Bright Side: Africa Is Building Its Own Cyber Defenders

Okay, deep breath, it's not all doom and gloom. Here's the genuinely exciting part: a new generation of African cybersecurity talent is rising fast, and governments and regulators are starting to treat cyber defense as a matter of national importance rather than an IT afterthought.

  • Regulation is catching up: Data protection laws are getting real teeth across the continent, pushing companies to treat compliance as a genuine safeguard instead of paperwork.
  • Conferences and summits are multiplying: Events across South Africa and beyond are bringing together government, industry, and researchers to build shared defense strategies.
  • Homegrown talent is stepping up: Local academies, bootcamps, and university programs are training the next wave of ethical hackers, analysts, and incident responders, the people who will close that workforce gap.
A new generation of homegrown talent is building Africa's cyber defenses from the ground up.

Essential Habits That Actually Protect You (No Tech Degree Required)

You don't need to be a security engineer to dramatically lower your risk. Here's what genuinely moves the needle, whether you run a business or just really like your phone not being hacked:

  1. Turn on multifactor authentication everywhere it is offered, even though it is mildly annoying.
  2. Never approve a payment based on a voice note or urgent message alone, call back on a known number.
  3. Update your apps and phone software, those boring updates usually patch real security holes.
  4. Use a password manager instead of "Password123" for the ninth account this year.
  5. Get an actual professional scan of your business systems instead of hoping for the best. (This is where we, admittedly, think VunVault can help.)

Where This Is All Heading

Put it all together and a clear picture emerges: Africa's cybersecurity story right now is really a race between two forces, the speed of digital adoption, and the speed of digital defense catching up to it. Right now, adoption is winning. But the gap is narrowing, fueled by new regulation, new training pipelines, and a growing public awareness that "it won't happen to me" is not a security strategy.

The organizations and individuals who treat cybersecurity as a habit, not a single project, are the ones who'll come out ahead. The rest will be the ones writing "we take security seriously" in a breach notification email.

Navigating Cybersecurity Compliance in Africa: A Practical Guide for Modern Enterprises

Published 29 Jul 2026 Reading Time 5 Minutes Author VUNVAULT Security Team

There was a time when cybersecurity compliance in Africa was treated as a voluntary measure: a badge of honour reserved only for multinational banks or global technology firms. Today, those days are firmly behind us. Compliance has officially become a matter of legal survival.

With governments across the continent rapidly enacting and enforcing robust data protection regulations, enterprises must transition from passive security promises to active, documented proof of defense. In this comprehensive guide, we demystify the African compliance landscape and outline actionable controls to ensure your business stays secure and audit ready.

A protective digital shield representing data privacy audits and legal standards.

The Evolving Regulatory Shield in Africa

Africa is witnessing a historic rise in regulatory oversight. Over the past several years, multiple countries have established fully functional data protection commissions with the power to issue severe financial penalties and suspend operating licenses.

Understanding which rules govern your business is the critical first step:

  • Kenya Data Protection Act: Overseen by the Office of the Data Protection Commissioner (ODPC), this act mandates strict registration for data controllers and processors, with fines of up to five million shillings for non compliance.
  • South Africa Protection of Personal Information Act (POPIA): Going into full effect with aggressive enforcement, POPIA demands that organizations implement strict physical and digital safeguards for all customer data.
  • Nigeria Data Protection Act (NDPA): Enacted recently to align Nigerian businesses with international standards, the NDPA requires annual data audits and a dedicated data protection officer for major data processors.
  • Sector Specific Mandates: Sector watchdogs like SASRA (Sacco Societies Regulatory Authority in Kenya) and central banks across the continent have instituted mandatory independent penetration testing schedules as licensing conditions.

"A company can buy the best firewalls in the world, but if they cannot show documentation of regular audits and vulnerability validation, they are still legally exposed."

Essential Compliance Requirements You Must Meet

While the laws vary slightly by jurisdiction, their technical requirements are remarkably consistent. Any enterprise aiming for alignment with international standards like PCI DSS, SASRA, or data protection acts must satisfy these essential technical pillars:

Rigorous Access Management

Data must be restricted on a need to know basis. Compliance auditors expect to see active multi factor authentication enforced across all administrative accounts and databases. Furthermore, user access rights must be periodically reviewed and instantly revoked when an employee leaves the organization.

Continuous Encryption standards

All personally identifiable information must be encrypted both in transit (while moving over public networks) and at rest (while stored in database servers or cloud storage buckets). Leaving API endpoints unencrypted or failing to utilize secure connection protocols is one of the most common findings leading to major regulatory penalties.

Documented Independent Testing

You cannot grade your own exam. Compliance frameworks across sub Saharan Africa explicitly demand regular, independent security assessments. Conducting a penetration test at least once a year by a certified third party provider is a standard prerequisite to satisfy central bank and data commission audits.

The Role of Offensive Security in Compliance

Many organizations believe that setting up a fire wall and installing antivirus software is enough to pass an audit. However, compliance is not just about having defenses; it is about verifying that those defenses actually perform in real world attack scenarios.

This is where professional penetration testing becomes invaluable. By legally mimicking the tactics of malicious hackers, an offensive security assessment provides the objective proof that auditors demand. A comprehensive pentest report does three essential things for your compliance audit:

  1. Validates Security Controls: Proves to regulators that your access controls, encryption, and firewalls are fully operational and capable of stopping active intrusions.
  2. Provides Documented Action Plans: Delivers a prioritized remediation roadmap that demonstrates your proactive commitment to risk management.
  3. Protects Executive Leadership: Safeguards board members and C level executives from personal liability by providing documented proof of cybersecurity due diligence.

Conclusion: Compliance as a Business Accelerator

Navigating compliance can often feel like an expensive bureaucratic hurdle. However, forward thinking businesses view it as a competitive advantage. In a digital market where trust is the primary currency, holding a clean audit report and verified security credentials is a powerful differentiator.

By shifting from a check box compliance mindset to a culture of continuous offensive testing and threat validation, your business does not just avoid regulatory penalties: it builds a legacy of trust that drives global expansion.

Shielding Africa’s Digital Renaissance: Why Penetration Testing is No Longer Optional

Published 29 Jul 2026 Reading Time 5 Minutes Author VUNVAULT Security Team

Africa is in the midst of an unprecedented digital revolution. Over the last decade, the continent has bypassed legacy banking infrastructures to pioneer mobile money (epitomized by systems like MPesa), witnessed an explosive fintech boom in tech hubs like Lagos, Nairobi, and Cape Town, and rapidly transitioned public and private systems to the cloud.

Yet, this rapid digital acceleration has caught the attention of a highly sophisticated global threat landscape. As African businesses and governments transition critical services online, they create massive new attack surfaces. Once considered an afterthought, offensive security, specifically penetration testing, has quickly evolved from a luxury compliance checkbox into an absolute operational necessity.

In this deep dive, we explore the state of penetration testing in Africa: the rising threat landscape, the regulatory forces shaping the market, the technical nuances of securing African infrastructures, and how businesses can navigate the path forward.

Cybersecurity nodes and digital shield overlays protecting the African continent.

The African Threat Landscape: A High Value Target

For many years, some businesses in Africa operated under a false sense of security, assuming global cybercriminals would focus on Western or Asian financial hubs. Today, that assumption is not only outdated; it is dangerous.

According to regional cybersecurity reports, African organizations face a disproportionately high rate of cyberattacks. The factors driving this include:

  • The Fintech and Mobile Money Boom: African fintechs handle billions of dollars in micro transactions weekly. These highly interconnected APIs and mobile applications make them prime targets for financial fraud and sophisticated API abuse.
  • Rapid Cloud Adoption: In the rush to digitalize, many organizations are migrating legacy systems to the cloud without establishing proper cloud security architectures, leading to exposed databases and misconfigured access permissions.
  • The Proliferation of Ransomware: Ransomware syndicates actively target critical infrastructure, utility providers, healthcare networks, and mid sized financial institutions across the continent, knowing that downtime can paralyze operations.

"In a landscape where security perimeters are constantly shifting, waiting for an incident to occur is a recipe for disaster. This is where penetration testing comes in."

What is Penetration Testing and Why Africa Needs It

Penetration testing or "ethical hacking" is the practice of legally and systematically probing an organization’s applications, networks, and cloud environments to find security vulnerabilities before malicious hackers do.

Unlike passive vulnerability scanning, which merely flags potential issues, a true penetration test involves active exploitation. A professional pentester acts exactly like an adversary: they attempt to bypass firewalls, crack credentials, manipulate business logic, and gain unauthorized access to sensitive systems.

A professional ethical hacker analyzing software vulnerabilities and security logs.

In Africa's highly dynamic digital ecosystem, penetration testing provides three invaluable benefits:

  1. Validating Real World Defenses: It proves whether your security controls actually work under simulated real world attacks.
  2. Uncovering Business Logic Flaws: Automated tools are excellent for finding outdated software, but they cannot understand context. A skilled human pentester can find logic flaws in custom built fintech applications, such as manipulating API parameters to transfer funds between accounts illegally.
  3. Preventing Catastrophic Financial Loss: Proactively identifying a vulnerability and fixing it costs a fraction of the price of responding to a full scale ransomware breach, data leak, or regulatory fine.

Market Growth and Regulatory Drivers

The demand for ethical hacking in Africa is growing exponentially. Market intelligence from MarketsandMarkets projects that the Middle East and Africa penetration testing market will grow from USD 200 million in 2025 to USD 370 million by 2031, at a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 10.9%.

This massive growth is fueled by two primary forces:

The Tightening Regulatory Grip

Governments across Africa are implementing robust regulatory frameworks modeled after Europe’s GDPR to safeguard citizen data. Notable examples include:

  • Kenya’s Data Protection Act (ODPC)
  • South Africa’s Protection of Personal Information Act (POPIA)
  • Nigeria’s Data Protection Regulation (NDPR)
  • Egypt’s Data Protection Law

These laws do not just penalize data breaches; they mandate proactive measures. Central banks and financial authorities across these nations increasingly require banks, microfinance institutions, and telecom operators to conduct quarterly or biannual independent penetration tests as a condition to maintain their operating licenses.

Cloud and Mobile First Technologies

As enterprises migrate to hybrid and multicloud environments, the demand for cloud security penetration testing is the fastest growing segment, projected to grow at a CAGR of 13.0%. Similarly, mobile application pentesting is soaring, driven by the sheer volume of Android centric mobile banking applications deployed across the continent.

Key Challenges Facing the African Pentesting Ecosystem

Despite the clear benefits and surging demand, the offensive security market in Africa faces several unique hurdles:

The Severe Cybersecurity Talent Gap

There is a massive global shortage of cybersecurity professionals, and Africa is hit particularly hard. While there is a highly enthusiastic grassroots community of ethical hackers, there is still a shortage of locally based, highly certified specialists holding industry standard credentials like OSCP (Offensive Security Certified Professional), CREST, or OSCE. As a result, many enterprises have historically relied on expensive Western or Middle Eastern consulting firms, though local talent is rapidly catching up.

Fragmented Compliance Across Borders

For pan African corporations operating across multiple countries (such as a fintech operating in Nigeria, Ghana, and Kenya), navigating differing regional compliance mandates can be a logistical headache. Each country has its own reporting timelines, breach notification policies, and testing frequencies, making a standardized security testing framework difficult to implement.

SME Budget Constraints

Small and medium sized enterprises (SMEs) are the backbone of Africa's economy, yet they often lack the capital to afford traditional, expensive manual penetration tests. To address this, Penetration Testing as a Service (PTaaS), which combines continuous automated scanning with on demand manual testing, is emerging as a highly scalable, cost effective alternative for growing businesses.

The Way Forward: Building a Proactive Cyber Culture

To secure Africa's digital future, organizations must shift their mindset from "compliance driven" security to "risk driven" security. Here is how forward thinking African enterprises are approaching pentesting:

  • Move Beyond "Once a Year" Testing: A penetration test is a snapshot in time. The moment a developer pushes new code or a network engineer opens a port, new vulnerabilities can emerge. Transitioning to a continuous security validation model ensures year round protection.
  • Invest in Manual Testing over Pure Automation: While automated scanners are useful for basic hygiene, human ingenuity is required to defeat human attackers. Research shows that manual penetration testing accounts for over 76% of the market share because it is unmatched at finding high impact, complex vulnerability chains.
  • Foster Local Talent: Organizations should actively collaborate with local universities, cybersecurity communities (like SheHacks Africa, AfricaHackon, or regional Bsides), and bootcamps. Nurturing homegrown talent is the only sustainable way to secure the continent’s infrastructure.

Conclusion: Securing the Digital Frontier

Africa’s digital economy holds limitless potential. However, this potential can only be fully realized if built on a foundation of trust, resilience, and robust security.

Penetration testing is no longer just a technical task managed by the IT department; it is a critical pillar of corporate governance. By proactively identifying vulnerabilities, respecting regional regulatory mandates, and investing in continuous security validation, African businesses can confidently innovate, protect their customer trust, and robustly defend the digital frontier.

Chinese Hacker Commands DeepSeek via Telegram to Launch Autonomous Attacks

A Chinese speaking threat actor wired DeepSeek into the open source Hermes Agent framework, issuing a single Telegram command and then letting the AI run an autonomous scan research exploit pipeline against more than 460 targets.
V
VUNVAULT News Desk
July 31, 2026 · ~7 min read
#AI Security#DeepSeek#Unit42#Autonomous Attacks#HermesAgent
DeepSeek-powered Hermes Agent orchestrating an autonomous offensive campaign — image via GBHackers

Palo Alto Networks' threat intelligence unit Unit 42 has documented what it describes as a functional, end to end autonomous offensive capability powered by an AI model. In a campaign attributed to a Chinese speaking actor operating under the aliases "knaithe" and "KnYuan" assessed by researchers to be based in Zhuhai, China DeepSeek was wired into the open source Hermes Agent framework to serve as an autonomous offensive operator.

According to the report, the workflow required the human operator to send a single Telegram instruction and then largely step back. Unit 42 recovered a complete session from May 2026 in which no additional operator input was found after the initial task: DeepSeek handled target enumeration, vulnerability selection, exploit sourcing from GitHub, and attack execution on its own. The actor launched exploitation attempts against more than 460 targets using both autonomous and conventional workflows.

"While the observed campaign had limited impacts, the workflow confirms a functional, end to end autonomous offensive capability." Unit 42

DeepSeek Was the Third Choice, Not the First

Notably, the actor did not begin with DeepSeek. Unit 42's report indicates the operator first approached Western AI models and that both Claude and OpenAI refused the offensive tasks. OpenAI reportedly went further, and its safety systems independently detected and disabled an account linked to the campaign before Unit 42 shared its intelligence. DeepSeek, however, did not refuse, which likely led the actor to select the most permissive model for the operation.

How the Autonomous Attacker Was Built

  • Hermes Agent an open source AI framework from Nous Research that supplied terminal access, a reusable skills system, unattended task execution, and Telegram based command and control.
  • Model Context Protocol (MCP) used to connect the model to external services and tools.
  • DeepSeek the primary reasoning model acting as the "brain" of the operation, with limited use of Claude Code and Qwen Code also observed.

Attack Path: From Langflow to n8n to Marimo

  • Downloaded a public exploit for the Langflow code injection flaw CVE-2026-33017, enumerated 84 instances via FOFA, and identified one target running version 1.3.4 but the attack stopped because the system had neither auto_login enabled nor a usable public flow identifier.
  • Surveyed 10 product families, searched GitHub for recent proof of concept repositories, and selected n8n. It obtained a chain combining the unauthenticated file access flaw CVE-2026-21858 with the expression injection issue CVE-2025-68613. FOFA returned 25,209 n8n systems in China; the agent sampled roughly 100, probed about 40, and identified three running vulnerable versions but no n8n system was ultimately compromised because targets required authentication.

Unit 42 concluded that version 1.121.1 is the earliest n8n release addressing both flaws used in the attempted chain, and that Marimo fixed CVE-2026-39987 in version 0.23.0.

Key Facts at a Glance

  • Actor: "knaithe" / "KnYuan" assessed to be in Zhuhai, China.
  • Vector: DeepSeek + open source Hermes Agent, controlled via Telegram.
  • Scale: 460+ targets; 7 exploit tracks; 8 CVEs.
  • Result: Autonomous campaigns largely failed; manual ops exfiltrated data from 3 organizations and executed commands on 11 Marimo instances.
  • Exposure: The agent accidentally started an HTTP server, leaking its own configs, API keys, target lists and session logs.
  • Refusals: Claude and OpenAI refused the tasks; OpenAI disabled a linked account.

The Manual Campaign Succeeded Where the AI Failed

The autonomous DeepSeek campaigns failed against targets with stronger default configurations. The operator's manual operations did not. Unit 42's report separately describes data exfiltration from three organizations and command execution on eleven Marimo instances. Researchers have not yet publicly reconciled these figures with the limited impact of the autonomous attacks.

How the Operation Was Exposed

One of the most unusual aspects of the case is how the operation was uncovered: Hermes Agent inadvertently launched python3 -m http.server 8888 from /home/worker, making the operator's model configurations, API keys, exploit scripts, target lists, shell history, and autonomous session logs publicly accessible. This accidental exposure gave Unit 42 investigators a rare, in depth view of the entire operation.

Mitigations and Recommendations

SystemAction
Langflow, n8n, MarimoPatch exposed instances; update n8n to ≥ 1.121.1 and Marimo to ≥ 0.23.0.
Citrix NetScaler ADC / GatewayPatch customer managed appliances configured as SAML identity providers (CVE-2026-3055).
Workflow & notebook interfacesRemove unnecessary public access; enforce authentication on all exposed endpoints.

Unit 42 warned that targets with weaker default configurations would have been susceptible, and emphasized that in this documented case, no detection fired and no alert was raised the AI campaign was only stopped by authentication configuration, not by monitoring.

The Bigger Picture: AI as an Autonomous Weapon

Security researchers describe the incident as one of the most prominent examples to date of large language models being used as autonomous attack tools that can operate without constant human supervision. It also highlights a legal and geopolitical dimension: because data submitted to DeepSeek's API may be subject to Chinese government access on demand, organizations evaluating DeepSeek for their own deployments face a fixed condition based on the model's jurisdiction of origin.
